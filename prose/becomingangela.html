Becoming Angela

Besides, she continued, he always spoke too much—about nothing, mostly. But not a buffoon, she conceded. She could hardly remember him. Paul—the wanker—had long dark hair, corduroy jeans, and a Trinity candour Angela liked. She loved to roll her eyes, hang off his arm, parade him about. He was the artist type, though studying to be an accountant. He wrote poetry. He was twenty- two.

Angela, only three months in Dublin, dating a man. Her school friends were wary at first, wide eyed and cautious. The illicit excitement of a four-year age gap flickered as she relayed her sophistication, his maturity.

Paul had brought her first year into blossom. On his arm, the world unfolded before her. She was gorgeous beside him, strolling down Grafton Street–though that was gaudy. Unless it was Christmas, Paul advised.

He brought her out, not to Camden Street or Coppers, but to parties in Portobello and then to Capel Street and other places on the North Side that none of her friends had heard of.

Over lunch with Molly and Sarah, she told them about the DJ set after the rave in that basement—and she hadn’t even intended on showing off.

Paul, they all agreed, had impeccable taste. The boys of their adolescence were crude, uncouth. They talked endlessly of the county team, wore jerseys like uniforms, spoke only of the familiar. Not one would order an oat flat white. Those ‘lads’ and that world seemed separate now, faded into a life before Paul.

Paul and Angela fell into easy routine. On Sundays, Paul would collect her in his car and drive them to his family home–an old Georgian redbrick in Glenageary. They spent long mornings chatting over coffee with his glamorous Mum, Audrey, as polished as her marble kitchen. The bakery run, 1pm lunch, the Forty foot if brazen; their habits soon became hers.

With a heralding cry, down the stairs would traipse Paul’s ultra-cool sister, Jennifer, melodramatic and deathly hungover. Jenny: her razor-sharp wit piercing even when slumped over at the kitchen table. She told stories you never minded listening to, even when they weren’t interesting. Jenny was painfully clever, seeming to sail through life with ease, adrift like a pretty cloud. Angela adored her. Angela–though half German and therefore a little exotic–occasionally felt out of place, busying herself with the vegetables as they discussed the vices of minimalist architecture and traded quips. Jenny relaying last night’s scandal, Audrey watching on with idle amusement.

Angela lived in a real-life secret garden. Every day brought intrigue–spontaneous plans, party invitations. The electric hum of possibility beating even as she still revelled in the aftermath of the night before. Yet, it was the end of the night out, the dwindling crowd, she most craved.

Wine buzzed, a cherry sour still tingling her lips, she’d loop her arm in Paul’s, kiss him freely. He’d swing her round, and she’d laugh, both stumbling, their friends ahead, heading toward some afters.

At Ahmad’s, they’d slip away to share a secret cigarette, perched up on the roof, the D8 silhouettes and its barley sky engulfing them.

“I think I’d like to go away, ditch it all for a bit, you know” Paul had said, looking out, stubbing the cigarette, frowning.

She pictured him, in a great city on the continent, painting miniatures, roaming foreign markets, put at ease by an eternal sun.

But here, beneath the blackened sky, the whites of his eyes searched hers, testing her.

“I’ll come with you” she’d said, sure of it then.

When they went to Luca’s, the gatherings were more discreet. Intimate. Here, amongst Ireland’s moneyed families, she was on edge–coiled, cat like, for reproval. Yet she was show dog too, offering complimentary affirmations, borrowed witticisms, never trite. She felt his eyes light up as he watched, brimming pleasure, animating her conversation, increasing her charisma.

"You really should try the Merlot," Paul said, pouring her a glass. "It's from a small vineyard in Bordeaux."

Or was it the Cab Sav? Really, who could taste the difference?

“Earthy” she ventured, eyeing the room for correction.

This was a different kettle—and she couldn’t risk misjudging it.

Jenny caught her eye across the table with an amused half-smile. Angela breathed out, relief and discomfort.

Angela worked hard; studied even. Parties became lecture theatres. She filed away every detail of conversation as a learning point. Each interaction became an opportunity, a test.

With many new friends and accumulating social homework, Angela found herself constantly occupied. She battled tutorial somnolence, haunted instead the library courtyard, rehashing the finer points of last night’s debate.

Her first semester was an academic disappointment, softened by social victory. Whilst her old friends were taking their first teetering steps as adults, giddy with novelty, Angela saw herself looking back, the older sibling.

She struggled to keep from patronising them, took care not to alienate them when she discussed European socialism, avoiding comprehensive discussion post ski trip. She only smiled, “You girls would really enjoy it”.

She seldom thought of home, guilty when she did pick up the phone. “How are you getting on!" Her dad's eager inquisition, his simple excitement and rural lilt that Angela despised in her own voice, though softened.

She swallowed hard before answering. "Settling in, yeah. Getting the hang of it..." The words plastic in her mouth, as if she were translating her new life into a language her father could understand. Her filial betrayal, yet unknown–half accidental, all unconscious.

"Yeah, no…I’m not sure. Yes, Molly’s well” she said, picking at a thread on her new jumper. She closed her eyes, hating how her voice sounded.

Her father always ended calls the same way, his dropping voice, earnest and suffocating.

When was she coming home? His favourite question.

Angela bit back irritation, turning to gaze out her bedroom window overlooking the campus courtyard where a group of students had congregated, laughter bellowing from below.

"I'm not sure yet. It's busy here."

She felt his hurt in the moment of silence that followed.

“Oh, that’s alright, love. Sure, let us know.”

His unconditional affection flattened her. Afterwards she cried, real tears, dredged up heart ache. She never wanted to join the others afterwards. Timed those phone calls well to avoid the detrimental buzz kill.

On rare trips back to Kilmeadan, she felt she visited in a dream. Bizarre to have known it so well. Even when she stood within it, Kilmeadan was hazy–like a foreign holiday photograph. Her connection to it seemed superficial and friable. She wandered through it on an acid trip of unreality.

The trees were stubby, practical and ubiquitous. The houses with their broken gutters and unpainted gates displeased her. The nearby Kilfarassy no longer impressed in its meagre prettiness. Sullen sea in the grey winter.

How had her parents come to settle in such a village? The presumed culprit–her father, Brendan. To be happy with so little. A small market for agricultural advisors in Dublin, no doubt.

Angela begrudged their decision, having fast realised on starting college that she was on the geo-cultural back-foot. She swore if she and Paul had kids, they would raise them in Dublin, to spare them.
