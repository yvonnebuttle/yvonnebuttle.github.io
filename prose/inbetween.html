In Between

I am a lone traveller. I stand on the deck of a ship bound north, though I am not the captain. Men move about me in rhythmic precision, a well-oiled platoon. I sit in committed, solemn observation, free from the constraints of human responsibility and decipher the darker depths of the ever-changing waters. They muddle together and merge as if they were Proteus fleeing discovery. Their secrets shimmer in the blinding Winter sunlight–untouchable yet magnificent. Aboard, I am suitably vulnerable to their splendour. I have seen many wonders on my travels, none yet so great as those offered to me by man.

And I, I rest irreverently between forms, nebulous and unstudied. I have the urge to flee their rash communications, their passionate displays of volatility and the kaleidoscopic world that is theirs. Wearing an unmarked appearance, I play the passer-by, the fellow diner in the restaurant. While others engage in tobacco-infused chat, I smoke alone, invisible within the cohort. When I go, I leave no trace behind, swept clean by wind and sea, a fleeting imprint.

The ship sways, dancing in the waves. A salty current of wind numbs my ears and clears my mind. There is a constant steadiness in nature; it has long weathered and accustomed itself to this planet, merging seamlessly with Earth’s very existence.

Humanity has but a fleeting moment of glory; their story will be tragic, but their legend long retold in languages indecipherable to themselves.

In the midst of these fragile, finite creatures, I now rise. It is supper hour. An old soul, his misty eyes rising from hollowed groves, beseeches us to assemble downstairs and eat. I follow. Mutely, his gaze greets mine. The daily humdrum, a sedating lullaby. I am fed, watered well with whiskey, and sent to bed. I return then to the small cubby hole, to my scant possessions and four cabin mates. The human pungency of this night is strong, and my head hurts.

Lying in the berth, sticky in an aromatic heat, I listen to the gentle snores of the old broad. Her maiden sighs punctuate her sleep, in memory of lost or imagined lovers. Her bonds are like mine but stronger, for mine are self-imposed. Emotion keeps her stationary and silent. Her mind lies awake, unsettled throughout the night whilst her aching limbs accept rest.

She is brash and vulgar in her beauty, yet her tenderness is not lost and in God’s good grace she remains. She is a living tragedy, a heroine of an unfair world. Romanticism values her in the best light, and so do I, being neither defined by nor objecting to its beliefs.

In the morning, I am dressed, breakfasted, and put sitting. The day is long, ominous, and the sky is as mercurial as the beings that observe it. Small problems prickle the day’s voyage, and I hear the bludgeonous sound of the anxious soften with the impressive cajoling of their trusted superiors. At dusk, the overplayed tragedies of lovers contrast well with the simplicity of death that taints the waters around the determined vessel. A warship carries with it plagues of death and glory, yet too it is a vessel for other things–a stage for conjecture of human nature nonetheless. Their world is a consequence of speculation and assumption. They live but in spite of themselves. It is the most beautiful thing. My words are raw; I cannot deceive as they do. I am a lone traveller, wandering a world that is not my own.

Below deck, brave men offer dances to ardent young women and those less brave or less desired leer in envy. An old Irish melody calls to me, her words sweet and soft to hear, but I am resigned to appreciation from afar. The beauty of their world is in their inability to understand or appreciate it. I will not stain their artless dance with my sobriety.

Still, I remain the solitary traveller. The wind is cold, the sky black. I accept the cloak of darkness, and stand alone.
